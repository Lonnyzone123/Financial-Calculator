## R38-01 — P1 — annual contribution caps are applied to annualized rates before part-year deposits

**Evidence and reach.** A valid, full age-45-to-46 projection row has retirement at 45.5, so employment and deposits last half a year. With zero return, inflation, fees and spending, the public-route witness reports:

| Input and independent expected deposit | Actual R38 deposit | Shortfall |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $10,000/year planned traditional IRA; half-year $5,000 is below the 2026 $7,500 annual IRA limit | $3,750 | $1,250 |
| $30,000/year planned 401(k) deferral; half-year $15,000 is below the 2026 $24,500 annual deferral limit | $12,250 | $2,750 |
| $300,000/year salary, $24,500/year deferral, 20% profit share: $12,250 + 20% of $150,000 = $42,250, below the $72,000 annual-additions ceiling | $36,000 | $6,250 |
| $500,000/year salary, $1,000/year deferral, 10% profit share: half-year pay is $250,000, below the $360,000 annual compensation ceiling; $500 + $25,000 = $25,500 | $18,500 | $7,000 |

The 2026 dollar amounts are in [IRS Notice 2025-67](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2025-49_IRB). The IRS explicitly says annual additions and compensation limits are **not prorated merely because a participant is eligible for part of a full limitation year**; proration concerns a [short limitation year](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/issue-snapshot-treatment-of-415c-dollar-limitations-in-a-short-limitation-year) or [short plan year](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/issue-snapshot-treatment-of-401a17-limitation-in-defined-contribution-plan-in-a-short-plan-year). These inputs model a full year with half-year employment, not a shortened employer plan year.

**Mechanism.** `src/engine.js:157` supplies full-year limits to `auditContributions()`. The allowed annual rate is then multiplied by `itemDuration` at `src/engine.js:3655`. The section 401(a)(17) compensation ceiling is applied to annual salary before duration at `:3668`, and the section 415(c) ceiling itself is multiplied by duration at `:3676`. The four witnesses isolate those three sites and are accepted by `validateScenario()`.

**Consequence.** The reported contribution, account class balance, later growth and retirement capacity are understated. Redirection and warning behavior can also change because excess is classified before the actual part-year deposit is known. The exact downstream amount depends on available accounts and the chosen limit policy; the table fixes zero returns and no taxable redirect destination to isolate the error.

**Proposed repair.** Compare each owner's *actual dollars deposited in the tax/plan year* with the unprorated annual cap, while preserving the intended work-duration scaling of the user's annual contribution rate and employer compensation. Treat a genuinely short plan or limitation year only when the model has an explicit basis for one. Add public-route regressions for all four figures and whole-year controls under both `redirect` and `warn` policies.

## R38-02 — P2 — Rule of 55 is denied after a fractional starting age

**Evidence and reach.** A valid single-person plan starts at 54.5, separates from its current employer at 55 and transfers $50,000 from that employer's traditional 401(k) at **55.5**, six months after separation. It has zero wages, returns, spending and dividends. At the age-56 row, R38 reports **$9,667.50** of tax. Independent arithmetic using the model's zero-indexed 2026 amounts is $3,820 federal ordinary tax plus $847.50 Arizona tax at the [statutory 2.5% rate](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/43/01011.htm), or **$4,667.50**. The extra **$5,000** is exactly 10% of the distribution. Setting only `penaltyException: true` produces the $4,667.50 control; starting at whole age 54 also avoids the error. The taxable closing balance is $5,000 too low in the fractional-start case.

**Mechanism.** `src/engine.js:2189` tests `Math.floor(left-start)+Math.floor(start)>=55`. At start 54.5 and separation 55, that expression is `0+54`, although the person separated at age 55. The [IRS qualified-plan separation exception](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-exceptions-to-tax-on-early-distributions) covers distributions after separation in or after the year the employee reaches 55. The transfer occurs later than separation, so no same-instant event ordering is at issue. The app's opening-age convention for pooled withdrawals does not describe this dated transfer.

**Proposed repair.** Derive the separation-year eligibility from the person's actual separation age/calendar-year model without discarding the fractional starting age. Add public transfer tests starting at 54, 54.5 and at least one earlier fractional age, with a pre-55 separation negative control and IRA/old-employer-plan negative controls.

## R38-03 — P2 — Roth match election uses entered vesting rather than effective vesting

**Evidence and reach.** A consistent six-year graded schedule starts at age 63 with five completed service years and `vesting: 80`. Salary is $100,000/year; a Roth 401(k) receives $6,000/year of employee deferral and a 100%-up-to-6% elected Roth match. The employee retires at 64.5. After the first full row, six completed service years make the match 100% vested under the engine's own `employerVestedShare()` rule. The second, half-year row earns a **$3,000** match. R38 instead leaves that $3,000 pretax: at age 65, the row shows **$9,000 pretax and $9,000 Roth**, where the pretax balance should be **$6,000** and the Roth account should receive the match before its additional tax funding. The row's current tax is $7,474; recognizing $3,000 of additional ordinary income in the modeled 12% federal plus 2.5% Arizona marginal band adds $435 before funding effects.

**Mechanism.** `src/engine.js:2569-2570` returns Roth only when the *entered* `vesting` equals 100. `employerVestedShare()` at `:2181` separately makes this account fully vested after service (and at age 65). The routing call at `:3680` reads the static test, so it never sees the achieved vesting. [IRS Notice 2024-2, section L, Q&A L-3](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2024-02_IRB) conditions an elected designated Roth employer contribution on full vesting when allocated. R38's handover discloses the opposite, inconsistent-input case (`vesting: 100`, `yearsOfService: 0`); it does not disclose this consistent transition to full vesting.

**Consequence and repair.** The account tax classes, current ordinary income, tax funding and later withdrawal taxes change. Route an elected match using the same effective vested state used by the allocation and forfeiture logic at that point in the owner's service and age clock. Test the transition from partial to full vesting, the age-65 path, and the existing inconsistent-input case explicitly.

## R38-04 — P2 — a claim inside a row can omit a COLA already earned at claim

**Evidence and reach.** A plan opens at age 66.5 with a $2,000 monthly Social Security PIA in today's dollars, 10% configured COLA, and claim at 67.5. It has no assets, wages, other income, return or spending. Claim is one year after plan start and halfway through the age-67-to-68 row. The expected paid monthly amount is `$2,000 × 1.10 × 1.04 = $2,288`: one preclaim COLA and six months of delayed retirement credit beyond FRA 67. Six paid months are **$13,728**. R38 reports **$12,480**, **$1,248 low**, corresponding to the delayed credit without the COLA. [SSA regulation 20 CFR 404.271](https://www.ssa.gov/OP_Home/cfr20/404/404-0271.htm) applies COLAs to the PIA before entitlement.

**Mechanism.** `src/engine.js:3063-3065` fixes `ssPiaAt()` at the row's opening age (67) for every segment of that row. `ssColaRates()` at `:2978` counts only whole years reached by that opening. The claim at 67.5 has completed the year from plan age 66.5, but the row opening has not. This is separate from the documented limitation about anchoring historical calendar COLAs before a plan begins.

**Proposed repair.** Price a benefit that begins inside a row at the claim segment's age, then keep the model's dime/dollar rounding and paid-month rules. Add a midrow claim witness and whole-row claim controls for both owners, including an age-gap spouse.

## R38-05 — P2, conditional — survivor's 401(k) retains deceased participant's current-employer flag

**Evidence and scope.** The succession loop at `src/engine.js:3619-3632` changes an account's owner but leaves its `currentEmployerPlan` flag unchanged. `rmdObligations()` at `:2889-2895` then treats an inherited 401(k) as the working survivor's *current employer* plan. In a valid public-route witness, both spouses start at 74, self dies at 74.5, spouse salary is $100,000 with retirement at 85, and the deceased owner has $100,000 in a 401(k) flagged as *that owner's* current-employer plan. At zero return, the death-year RMD is $3,921.57, but the survivor's next-year RMD is **$0** on roughly $96,078. Changing only the receiving account type to the survivor's own IRA gives **$3,905.63** at age 75: `(100,000 − 100,000/25.5)/24.6`, using the [IRS Uniform Lifetime Table](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590b).

**Decision and consequence.** A surviving spouse can choose among permitted rollover destinations; an accepting current-employer plan could yield a different RMD result under [IRS beneficiary and RMD rules](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-beneficiary). The app collects no destination election. Its `SPOUSAL_ROLLOVER_ASSUMED` warning says the survivor takes the account as their own but does not explain that the deceased participant's employer flag can suppress the survivor's RMD. The owner should decide an assumed destination, or require a warning/refusal when the destination affects the RMD, before Claude repairs this path. **$3,905.63 is due in the own-IRA alternative, not under every possible rollover.**
